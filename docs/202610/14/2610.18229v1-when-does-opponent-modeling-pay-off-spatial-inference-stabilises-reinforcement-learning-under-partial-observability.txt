# When Does Opponent Modeling Pay Off? Spatial Inference Stabilises Reinforcement Learning under Partial Observability 

## Marcel Petran 

Faculty of Mathematics and Physics, Charles University Prague, Czech Republic 

## Stefan Edelkamp 

Faculty of Mathematics and Physics, Charles University Prague, Czech Republic 

Abstract 

Deep reinforcement learning agents in partially observable com-petitive games must act on beliefs about an unseen opponent’s intent. We study a spatial opponent model: a transformer trained with hindsight-relabelled opponent goals that outputs a subgoal heatmap from the opponent’s observed trajectory, fused early into a dueling double DQN. In symmetric constant-sum foraging games with partial observability, the module stabilises training. Plain TD(0) DQN suffers degenerate seeds in three of four one-step configu-rations, whereas the opponent-model agent never collapses and, wherever training succeeds, recovers the majority of the symmetric game value. Transfers zero-shot to a foraging opponent unseen by the learner, with a positive goal advantage over the baseline, and loses this edge exactly where an information argument predicts: against a full-information tracking adversary whose intent is the learner itself, and on resource-dense maps where both agents sat-urate. A blindfold ablation, zeroing the heatmap at deployment, decomposes the advantage into two mechanisms: training-time stabilisation, which dominates where the credit assignment is hard and survives blinding on the dense map, and deployment-time in-ference, whose reliance shrinks as fog thins on the sparse map. We conclude that opponent-goal inference is a stability-and-transfer mechanism whose value is governed by measurable boundary con-ditions: visibility (fog thickness), opponent intent, and resource density. 

Keywords 

opponent modeling, partial observability, multi-agent reinforce-ment learning, goal recognition

Instead, a prominent approach is to model the adversary directly, actively inferring their underlying strategies, intentions, or goals to antici-pate future states. This approach is generally known as Opponent Modeling (OM). OM is usually evaluated only as “beats the base-line,” so we ask when it helps. The spatial opponent model and its ESS formalism were introduced in prior work [16] for Fully Observable Stochastic Games (FOSG) [12]. Here we adapt it to partial observability, replacing privileged inputs with the fogged observation history and its first-order motion encodings, and fusing the inferred heatmap alongside learned belief channels. 2 Related Work 

Opponent modeling has a long history, from theory-of-mind-style agent modeling [2, 17, 18] to game-specific predictors [4, 8]. Nashed and Zilberstein survey opponent-modeling methods and their de-sign axes [14]. Closest to us, Yu et al. infer opponent subgoals and integrate them into policy learning [23]; their subgoal inference conditions on the full state, whereas we infer a spatial intent dis-tribution from partial observations, and ask when the inference channel pays off rather than whether it improves a benchmark. Goal and plan recognition share our prediction target [5]; our hindsight-relabelled transformer (Section 4) is a lightweight online variant of such methods, using realised opponent outcomes as supervision in the spirit of HER [3]. Under partial observability, agent modeling has been tackled with recurrent opponent embeddings [15], Bayes-adaptive meta-learners [24], and policy-inference networks [10]; these predict opponent policies or types , not their spatial intent. Our games are partially observable stochastic games [7], and our contributions are orthogonal to MARL algorithmics: we modify the observation pipeline of a standard dueling DDQN [13, 21, 22] and measure the effect on training stability and transfer.

Explicit Sequential Subgoal Games. The ESS formalism and the spatial opponent model below were introduced in prior work [16]; we summarise them for completeness and describe our partial-observability adaptation. We now characterise the class of games for which inferring a spatial subgoal is the right prediction target. Many tasks are hierarchical: before winning, an agent must complete a sequence of intermediate objectives, or subgoals . Predicting the opponent’s full future state is unnecessarily noisy: the state carries contextual information that constrains movement but does not define the objective. We therefore decompose the state space as a Cartesian product S = P × Q where P is the subgoal feature space ,the state variables whose values complete the atomic objective (e.g. an agent’s coordinates), and Q is the context feature space , variables that constrain behaviour but do not define the objective (walls, free space). 4 The Spatial Opponent Model under Partial Observability 

The Spatial Opponent Model (Spatial OM) was introduced for fully observable games in prior work [16]. Relative to that instantiation, partial observability requires three changes: (i) inference over a sliding window of fogged observations rather than the full state, (ii) motion-difference channels as the primary sighting signal, and (iii) offline pre-training; in preliminary runs without it, the fusion channel is ignored by the Q-network and does not recover. The prediction target is unchanged: the adversary’s physical destination as a dense heatmap over the grid, with the ESS formalism restricting predictions to meaningful destinations. Pre-training the opponent model. If the Spatial OM and the Q-network are trained concurrently from random initialisation, the early heatmaps are indistinguishable from uniform noise; the Q-network’s CNN quickly learns to assign near-zero weights to the heatmap channel, and once ignored the channel rarely recovers. We therefore pre-train 𝑝 𝜙 offline before joint RL training. A supervised dataset of trajectories is collected by simulating games between heuristic agents (various pairings of the simple and greedy profiles, Section 5) and labelled via hindsight labelling (Algorithm 1). 6 Results 

Training stability. Plain DQN is unstable at 𝑛 =1 (Figure 6). On map4 one classic seed collapses outright in 𝑣 =2 and all three 𝑣 =5seeds finish at 37–46% of game value (1.3–1.6 of 3.5); on map3 at thick fog all 𝑛 =1 seeds finish at 47–68% of game value (1.17–1.71 of 2.5). om never collapses and never finishes below 80% of game value on map3 (floor 2.03 of 2.5). Across all zero-shot batches on both maps, seed-level standard deviation is 1.4–3.6 × smaller for om (map4: 0.16–0.31 vs. 0.50–0.79). 

In-distribution performance. Against the training opponent (Fig-ure 7), om recovers 95% (map4, 𝑣 =2) to 99.9% ( 𝑣 =5) of the game value in well-trained ( 𝑛 =3) cells; classic reaches 77–88%. On map3 both agents reach 96–101% at 𝑛 =3, since the game is saturated. Under thin fog both even exceed the game value on average (104% for om, 101% for classic; individual seeds up to 107%): a best response to the suboptimal training heuristic. Thick fog separates them: in the hard cell ( 𝑣 =2, 𝑛 =1) classic degenerates to 52–76% per seed (64% on average), because exploiting an opponent you rarely see requires predicting it. 

Opponent spectrum. The advantage transfers across forager types, seen or unseen, and in well-trained scenarios disappears against the tracker (Table 2). simple is unseen by the learner, but its profile appears in the OM’s offline pre-training corpus (Section 4), so this tests the learned policy’s transfer, not the OM’s. This fits an infor-mation argument. A forager’s intent is a fact about the environment: its trajectory reveals it, and the learner’s fogged observations do not contain it. 7 Limitations 

Three seeds per configuration. We report per-seed strip plots and never pool across 𝑛 -step configurations (except where stated, as in the belief-prior comparison), and several cells have seed-level stan-dard deviations comparable to their means and should be read as non-significant. One classic seed on map4 collapsed during train-ing and is excluded from paired statistics there, with the training curve cited as justification. Collapse frequency is itself reported. All results are best responses to a heuristic. In preliminary experiments, NFSP-style self-play [9] did not converge in the FOSG domain, so we make no MARL equilibrium claims. Published opponent-modeling methods assume different observation spaces (full state, action his-tories, or population priors). None infers spatial opponent subgoals from partial observations in this setting, so we compare against classic, whose Q-network matches the om Q-network on obser-vation and belief channels and on capacity, differing only in the heatmap input, although that heatmap summarises an observation history classic does not see (see Conclusion). We fix the learner to value-based DQN methods because the claim concerns TD(0) stability under partial observability. Policy-gradient learners would change the failure mode rather than test it. Zeroing the heatmap is an input distribution shift. The drop measures information loss plus input corruption, not information loss alone. And lastly, this work focuses on grid foraging only, scale is future work. 

8 Conclusion and Future Work 

Opponent-goal inference under partial observability is not a uni-form advantage: its value is set by visibility (fog thickness), by whether the opponent’s intent is about the environment or about the learner, and by resource density. Its most robust benefit is mak-ing DQN training stable where plain TD(0) fails. We attribute the stabilisation to the module as a whole. Whether it stems from subgoal inference or from history-derived structured input is not isolated here. The blindfold ablation offers partial evidence: zeroing the heatmap, the only input beyond the channels shared with clas-sic, costs the most deployment performance in the cells where the information argument says inference matters most (in distribution and at thick fog on the sparse map), consistent with the inference carrying information rather than acting as generic structured in-put. Future work scales these mechanisms to roadmap graphs of real-world maps, where foraging under fog becomes a multi-agent travelling-salesman problem under partial observability, in both competitive and cooperative settings, and replaces self-play with policy-space or population methods for equilibrium approximation.
